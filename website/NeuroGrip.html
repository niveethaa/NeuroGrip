A steady grip for the moments that matter.

NeuroGrip is a soft, sensor-guided brace for people living with ALS. It helps the thumb and index finger close around a pen, a marker or a spoon, keeps the grip steady, and adjusts its support through the day.

Holding

Keeping a grip is the hard part

He can start a pinch easily, but holding it gets tiring within seconds, and writing tires after about ten minutes.

Time of day

Steadier in the morning

He has more energy at 9 AM than at 4 PM; his reading pace shifts from 165 to 118 words a minute.

Over time

Needs that change month to month

Both hands are changing gradually, so support has to adapt with him.

Why support, not repair
Electrical stimulation

Relies on the nerve pathway ALS affects

Stimulation works through the motor nerve, which his nerve studies show is affected.

Strength training

Exercise can't replace nerve signals

Gentle activity helps wellbeing, but it can't restore the signals a pinch depends on.

Fixed-assist devices

One setting can't fit his day

The right amount of help in the morning is different in the afternoon, and over the months.

Our solution

NeuroGrip lends the steadiness, Alex stays in control

1 · Touch

The forefinger finds the pen

A touch sensor on the forefinger feels the pen, which tells NeuroGrip where it is.

2 · Measure

The angle sensor plans the grip

An analog angle sensor at the knuckle reads the finger position and works out how far the thumb should move.

3 · Hold

The thumb closes

The Arduino tells the servo motor how much pressure to apply, and the exoskeleton presses the thumb against the pen.

4 · Release

He lifts his pinky to let go

While he writes, his pinky rests on a palm sensor. Lifting it even slightly tells NeuroGrip to release the tendon slowly.

Our prototype

Built this weekend, and why it works

A fabric glove exoskeleton with a cable tendon to the thumb, a servo motor on the forearm and three sensors, controlled by an Arduino.

Palm side. Touch sensors sit on the forefinger and under the pinky; the angle sensor sits on the back of the hand.

Tendons, not motors in the hand

It copies his own anatomy

Finger muscles live in the forearm and pull tendons. NeuroGrip's servo motor sits on the forearm and pulls a cable tendon to the thumb the same way, so the hand stays light.

Holds without effort

The servo applies steady pressure

Once the forefinger finds the pen, the servo presses the thumb in and keeps the grip steady, so Alex can save his energy for writing.

Measures before it moves

The angle sensor sets the thumb

Reading the finger position, it works out how far the thumb should move and how much pressure to apply.

Lets go naturally

Lift the pinky to release

In a natural writing grip, his pinky rests on the palm sensor. Lifting it is the only way the grip lets go, and the tendon loosens slowly so nothing drops suddenly. Force is capped, and a caregiver can open it with a backup button.

What Tiger Data sees

The Arduino decides each grip. Tiger Data remembers all of them.

Every grip is stored with a timestamp. Over days and months, the trend helps the brace adjust and gives his ALS clinic a clearer picture. Next, the Gemini API will analyze this history to fine-tune sensor sensitivity.

ALS Canada equipment support, which Alex was referred to, and Ontario's Assistive Devices Program.

Safety

Lets go only when he chooses

Capped force, a slow release that starts only when he lifts his pinky, and a caregiver backup button.
